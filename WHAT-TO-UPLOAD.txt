1. WHAT YOU OWN
---------------
  A laptop bought outright is not a cost in the month it was bought; it is a
  cost spread over the years it is used. Recorded as a cost on the day, the
  month looks far worse than it was and the next three look better — and it is
  the adjustment an accountant makes by hand every year end.

  The register holds what each thing cost, what it has worn down by and what
  it is worth now, pro-rated by days in the year it was bought. Depreciation
  posts into the books on the last day of each finished year, never for a year
  still running. Selling one clears the cost and the wear and books the gain
  or loss.

  Two columns of figures on purpose. The books spread a cost over the life of
  the thing; the tax computation pools assets into blocks and writes each
  block down at its own rate — and anything bought with fewer than 180 days
  left in the year gets half that rate, which is the rule most often missed. A
  sale is not a gain for tax at all: the proceeds come off the block. The
  screen says all of this rather than leaving somebody to wonder which figure
  is wrong.

  Large bills filed as costs are flagged as things that might be assets, and
  recording one keeps the bill where it is — nothing is counted twice.

  Lives and rates are read from published summaries, not the schedules
  themselves, so every class carries its source and is marked unverified.

2. YEAR END
-----------
  Nothing in these books is a stored balance, so closing a year is not
  arithmetic — it is somebody satisfying themselves the year is complete and
  saying so. The useful part is therefore the list of what is usually wrong
  when somebody thinks a year is finished: invoices still in draft (which
  means the year is understated), bills sitting in general expenses, large
  purchases that never reached the asset register, and books that do not
  balance. Drafts and an unbalanced sheet stop a close; the rest are flagged
  and do not hold you up.

  The balance sheet now splits profit kept from earlier years from profit made
  this year, instead of showing one figure covering every year you have
  traded. Closing sets the period lock to the year end.

3. ORDERS AND BILLS
-------------------
  A purchase order is a promise to pay for a particular thing at a particular
  price. The bill that arrives later is somebody else's account of the same
  transaction, and the two disagree more often than anybody expects.

  Bills can now be linked to the order they came from — the form offers that
  supplier's open orders — and the screen shows what was ordered against what
  was billed, what is still committed, and anything billed above the order.

  It also catches the same bill entered twice, matched on the supplier and
  bill number rather than the amount, because two bills for the same amount in
  a month is ordinary and the same number twice is not. Paying a bill twice is
  one of the few mistakes that costs real money and leaves no trace: the books
  balance either way, the supplier is simply better off.
